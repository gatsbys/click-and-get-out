The town that decided to turn the noise down

Over fourteen months, a council of 80,000 people stripped eight hundred advertising structures from its streets. What was underneath surprised even the people who had voted for it.

Nadia Faulkner| Chief reporter| 14 Sept 2026 · 18:42| 9 min read

On the first day nobody noticed. The crews started at six in the morning at the south end, where the road narrows, and by the time the shops opened they had taken down forty-three structures. Mid-afternoon, a woman walked into the council offices to ask whether something had fallen over.

“It happened to all of us,” admits the councillor who pushed the by-law through. “We had spent so long looking past that stuff that we had stopped seeing it. And then suddenly there is the front of a 1904 building that had been there the whole time, behind a phone company hoarding.”

We did not remove advertising. We removed a layer that had been up for thirty years and that nobody could remember approving.

Council report, page 11

The report published in June counts eight hundred and twelve structures removed, two hundred and nine of which had no current licence. Another hundred and forty took up more pavement than the rules allowed. The rest were legal, quite simply, because they had been legalised by accumulation.

What was underneath

The most quoted part of the report is not the numbers but the photographs. A hundred and sixty pages of before and after in which the “after” rarely adds anything: there simply stops being something. A brick arch. A plaque from 1936. Three plane trees that had spent a decade competing with a nine-metre billboard.

Local traders, who had signed a petition against the plan, published their own figures in May. Footfall on the central stretch rose eleven per cent between February and April. The association notes that the figure coincides with two adjacent streets being partly pedestrianised, and asks for caution before crediting a single cause.

What nobody disputes is the noise. Not the audible kind: the other one. In perception surveys, the share of residents describing the centre as “overwhelming” fell from thirty-one to fourteen per cent in a year. No building work took place in that time. Not one new tree was planted.

The cost

The operation cost £1.4 million, and the lost advertising revenue runs to about £380,000 a year. It is the argument repeated by the two groups who voted against, and the councillor does not deny it: “It is real money. What we are arguing about is what it buys.”

Five neighbouring councils have asked for a copy of the file. None has yet announced that it will do the same.
